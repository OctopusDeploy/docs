Decision document

Replacing the docs search

Two candidates, measured against the same corpus through the same search overlay. The engine in production today is shown throughout as the bar to clear, not as an option — it is being replaced either way.

3 query sets, 245 queries 2,364 searches recorded, 0 failed 3 performance runs, medians reported Fast 4G, 4× CPU throttle Run 19 Aug 2026
Recommendation

Ship Pagefind.

It wins on both sets built from real data. On the search terms readers actually typed it edges Orama 81% to 80%; on the pages readers actually visit it wins decisively, 98% against 90%, and puts the right page first 89% of the time against 79%. If a reader can name what they are after, Pagefind finds it.

It also costs a fraction as much. 0.08MB on the wire against 0.66MB, and 0.11MB parsed per page load against 5.33MB — a fiftieth. It starts faster from cold, it is nowhere near the CDN compression ceiling Orama sits two thirds of the way toward, and it is the only candidate that can deep-link to the heading that matched rather than the top of the page.

The cost
Typing lags. Pagefind searches WASM and decompresses index chunks per query, so about one keystroke in twenty waits close to a second — 927ms at p95 even on a 100Mbps desktop, against Orama's 184ms. Its median is 346ms, so this is intermittent stalling rather than uniform slowness.
It also cannot spell
42% on misspellings against Orama's 67%, and it fails by returning a confident wrong answer rather than nothing. varibles gets an Azure subscription page; a keyboard mash gets three security articles.
Do this before shipping
Raise the overlay's 150ms debounce and consider not searching under three characters. Pagefind's cost is per query, so firing fewer queries attacks the problem directly — and the logged terms show readers type fragments, most of which are not worth a round trip.

Orama's clearest win, and the strongest argument against this recommendation. The logged terms are dominated by fragments — var, tent, permiss, exter — because the property is captured per keystroke, so a good deal of the time the panel is open it is showing results for something half-typed. Orama leads by 15 points there and answers each keystroke five times faster.

What tempers it: readers finish typing. On the completed real terms the two are level at 80% and 81%, so the gap is in the intermediate states rather than the answer. Raising the debounce, or not searching below three characters, removes most of the fragments from the comparison entirely — and would cut Pagefind's per-query cost at the same time.

Evidence — speed

How fast does it feel?

Fast 4G with a 4× CPU penalty — roughly a mid-range Android on a decent mobile connection, and a deliberately pessimistic profile. Medians of three consecutive runs, measured against staging.

Staging is not production. Both spike sites are Azure static website endpoints: no compression, no CDN caching. Production is Azure Front Door, which returns Content-Encoding: br for this route. The compression-adjusted column scales bandwidth by each index's own brotli ratio and is an estimate, taken as the median of three runs. Everything else on this page is measured.

First result and keystroke cost — medians of three runs
Engine Cold, staging Cold, adjusted Warm Return visit Keystroke p95
Orama 5706ms 3950ms 326ms 288ms 324ms
Pagefind 2019ms 1821ms 1140ms 362ms 1186ms
Legacy — baseline 699ms 699ms 486ms 482ms 307ms

Read the row, not the cell. Orama is the slowest to answer the first search and the fastest at everything after it. Pagefind is the reverse of what its tiny index suggests: it starts quickest of the two but never gets faster, because it fetches index chunks per query and a query nobody has typed cannot be prefetched.

The keystroke column is the price of the recommendation. Orama's 324ms and legacy's 307ms sit near the floor set by the overlay's own 150ms debounce. Pagefind's 1186ms is roughly four times that, and it is paid on every keystroke rather than once — the one place where choosing Pagefind makes the reader's experience worse rather than better.

Run-to-run spread was under 90ms on every figure except Orama's cold start, which varied 5667–6826ms, and Pagefind's keystroke p95, which varied 1001–1516ms.

This table exists because the keystroke gap looked like it might be an artefact of the throttle, which would have made it dismissable. It is not. Taking Pagefind from Fast 4G to a 100Mbps desktop with no CPU penalty improves its p95 by 14%, from 1078ms to 927ms, while Orama falls to 184ms — the overlay's own 150ms debounce plus change. The cost is WASM search and chunk decompression, which bandwidth does not help.

The fair reading is that Pagefind stalls intermittently rather than being uniformly slow: its median is 346ms where its p95 is 927ms. About one keystroke in twenty waits close to a second. Orama's median and p95 are seven milliseconds apart.

The cold start

Neither number means what it looks like

Both engines start loading before the reader searches, but at different moments. Pagefind warms on page load; Orama warms on intent — hovering or tabbing to the search field. In the cold figures above neither gets much benefit from that, because the measurement searches as soon as the page is up. The table below gives them the head start a real reader would.

Time to first result by head start — compression-adjusted
Head start Orama Pagefind
None 3950ms 1821ms
1 second — 1199ms
2 seconds 1878ms 1209ms
5 seconds 298ms 1442ms

The shapes are opposite and that is the whole story. Orama converges to instant: give it the few seconds a reader spends moving to the field and typing, and the first search is 298ms. Pagefind flattens out around 1200ms and stays there no matter how long it has had, because the work it still has to do begins when the query exists.

So Orama's 3950ms describes one specific reader: the one who hits Ctrl+K and types instantly with a cold cache. Pagefind's 1200ms describes everyone, forever.

  • Orama has the least room to grow. Azure Front Door only compresses responses between 1 KB and 8 MB. Orama's index is 5.33MB uncompressed, two thirds of the way to that ceiling. Crossing it stops compression silently and returns the cold start to its uncompressed 5706ms. Pagefind's 0.11MB has no such problem. Whichever ships, the index size wants a build-time assertion.
  • Compression depends on caching being on. Front Door only compresses when caching is enabled for a route. It is enabled today, but the two settings are coupled — turning caching off for /docs/ would also turn compression off, and the adjusted column above would become the staging column.
  • Pagefind's keystroke cost is not network-bound. It was assumed to be, and tested: a 100Mbps connection with no CPU throttle improves its p95 by 14% and leaves it five times Orama's. The work is WASM search and chunk decompression, which a better connection does not help. The gap holds on every profile measured.
For whoever is choosing

What you gain and give up

Pagefind — recommended

  • Wins both real-data sets: 81% on real terms, 98% on top pages
  • 89% of top pages at rank one, against 79%
  • 0.08MB on the wire, 0.11MB parsed — an eighth and a fiftieth of Orama
  • Fastest first search of the two: 1821ms adjusted
  • Deep-links to the matching heading, which Orama cannot do
  • Enormous headroom before the 8MB compression ceiling
  • 927ms at p95 per keystroke even on a fast desktop; 346ms median
  • 42% on misspellings, and confidently wrong rather than empty
  • Returns three results for a keyboard mash

Orama

  • 184ms per keystroke on a desktop — the list keeps up with typing
  • 72% on partial queries, 15 points clear
  • Corrects misspellings: 67% against 42%
  • Best on dotted and punctuated input: 75% against 50%
  • Returns nothing for nonsense rather than inventing an answer
  • 3950ms first search with no head start, even compressed
  • 5.33MB parsed per page load; two thirds of the CDN compression ceiling
  • 8 points behind on top pages, 10 behind at rank one
  • No heading-level deep links

Both, against the baseline

  • 24–25 points better than legacy on real search terms
  • Delete 645 lines of hand-rolled stemmer, synonyms and ranking
  • Solve ambiguous titles outright, where legacy manages 90%
  • Index body text, which legacy never did
  • Neither handles non-English queries, which are in the log
  • Neither ships the search analytics the plan specified, so nothing will tell you whether the change worked
Decision levers

What would change the recommendation

The typing lag proves annoying in real use
Switch to Orama. This is the one axis where Pagefind makes things worse, and it is architectural rather than configurable: 927ms at p95 even on a 100Mbps desktop against Orama's 184ms. Try the debounce change first — fewer queries is the only lever Pagefind has — but if it still feels slow, that is a real reason to reverse this.
Misspellings turn out to be common in the logs
Switch to Orama, or add query expansion. Pagefind manages 42% on typos against Orama's 67%, and fails by inventing an answer rather than admitting none. A synonym and correction layer in the shared seam would close part of it for either engine, and is the smaller change.
Confident wrong answers are worse than none
Reconsider, or add a minimum score threshold. Pagefind returns three results for sssieddqxsx, the single most-typed term in the log. Orama returns nothing. Suppressing low-scoring results would fix it without changing engines.
Nobody adds the search analytics
You will not know whether this worked. Neither spike ships the events the plan specified, and the legacy equivalent was already dead. Whichever engine ships, this is the thing that turns the next version of this document from inference into measurement.
Method and limits

How to read this, and what it does not tell you

  • All three run the same overlay. The search UI merged before either spike branched, so the only thing that differs is the engine behind one interface. Both candidates are fully configured — this is not an untuned engine against a tuned one.
  • Three query sets, in descending order of authority. Real search terms from the log (57 scoreable, weighted by searchers); top-visited pages (98, weighted by 12,558 visitors); constructed behaviour probes (88 scoreable). Where they disagree, the real terms win — and they are the set on which the two candidates tie.
  • Expected pages are inferred throughout. The log records what was typed, analytics records what was visited, but nothing records whether a search succeeded. Every relevance number rests on a judgement about what the reader wanted, made before the engines were run and checked against the live page list.
  • Search is rare. 336 visitors used it against 12,620 who did not. Every ratio here sits on a small base, and the largest single real query has 17 searchers — a keyboard mash.
  • Corpus counts differ slightly. Pagefind indexes 1,251 pages, Orama 1,254, the legacy index 1,250. The gap is small but the engines are not indexing identical sets.
  • Not measured: peak memory on a real low-end device, behaviour on a flaky connection, and either candidate on a genuinely compressing host. The compression-adjusted figures are estimates from scaled bandwidth, not measurements.
